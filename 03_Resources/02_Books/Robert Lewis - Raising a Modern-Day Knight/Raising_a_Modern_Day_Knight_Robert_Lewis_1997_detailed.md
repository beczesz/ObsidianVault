# Raising a Modern‑Day Knight — Robert Lewis (1997)

## Context

**Author bio.** Robert Lewis is an American pastor, author and speaker.  He founded the Men’s Fraternity movement and served for more than twenty years as teaching pastor at Fellowship Bible Church in Little Rock, Arkansas【353121414343252†L780-L790】.  Lewis has written numerous books on marriage and family and was recognized as Pastor of the Year in 2001【843377090614210†L31-L40】.  His work focuses on helping fathers cultivate godly character and pass on a clear vision of manhood to their sons.

**Historical context.** *Raising a Modern‑Day Knight* was first published in 1997 and has since become a classic in Christian men’s ministries.  The book responds to a perceived crisis of masculinity in late‑20th‑century North America.  Lewis argues that many boys grow up without a clear model of manhood because absent or distracted fathers and an increasingly secular culture strip them of male identity【316853119993767†L200-L223】.  Drawing inspiration from medieval knighthood, he proposes a structured process of training and ceremonies to guide boys into responsible, godly manhood.

**Reception and influence.**  The book has been widely used by churches and men’s groups and is praised by evangelical leaders and politicians.  Endorsements from figures such as Mike Huckabee and Dennis Rainey appear at the beginning of the text【316853119993767†L0-L29】.  The success of the book spawned a video curriculum and companion resources, and its concepts have been incorporated into father‑son retreats and discipleship programs.

## Abstract

Lewis begins by recounting a father’s moment of realization: watching his teenage son he suddenly sees the boy becoming a man and wonders *what kind of man?*  Contemporary culture offers little guidance, viewing masculinity as problematic rather than noble【316853119993767†L208-L217】.  Many fathers lack the tools to define manhood, provide a developmental process, or mark their sons’ passage with ceremony【316853119993767†L352-L379】.  Lewis argues that *manhood must be bestowed*; it does not “just happen.”  He adapts the medieval model of knighthood—apprenticeship under a mentor, a clear code of conduct and rites of passage—to present three “ideals” for raising modern‑day knights: **a vision for manhood**, **a code of conduct**, and **a transcendent cause**【316853119993767†L1610-L1623】.  Fathers are urged to reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously and expect God’s reward【316853119993767†L1616-L1623】.  Through stories, historical anecdotes and practical guidelines, Lewis shows how fathers can craft ceremonies to celebrate milestones, enlist a community of men to support their sons, and leave a legacy of character.  Later chapters describe the results of the author’s own experiment with two friends in raising seven boys and offer encouragement to fathers who fear they have failed.  Ultimately, the book calls dads to reclaim their role as primary mentors, model godly manhood and initiate their sons into mature responsibility.

## Detailed Chapter Analysis

### Chapter 1 – **Manhood: Don’t Let Your Son Leave Home Without It**

**Key question:** How can a father ensure that his son leaves home with a clear understanding of what it means to be a man?

**Summary:** Lewis imagines a father hugging his 18‑year‑old son goodbye and wonders whether the boy knows what a man is【316853119993767†L278-L301】.  Through the story of Sam Rayburn, whose father simply said “Be a man” when he boarded a train, Lewis highlights the dilemma of urging manhood without definition.  He identifies three critical failures of modern fathers: (1) not providing an inspiring, biblically grounded *definition* of manhood; (2) lacking a *directional process* to help sons embrace manhood; and (3) neglecting *ceremonies* that commemorate progress【316853119993767†L352-L379】.  Without these assets, boys enter adulthood confused and directionless.  Lewis argues that a father must articulate a concrete vision, guide his son through practical steps and mark milestones with meaningful rituals.

**Examples:** The chapter recounts *Sam Rayburn’s story*—his father’s simple admonition “Sam, be a man!” and the lifelong influence of those words【316853119993767†L333-L350】.  Lewis also recalls his own experience: as a “family expert” he couldn’t answer a mother’s questions about raising sons【316853119993767†L200-L210】, prompting his journey to discover and share a process.

**Related chapters:** Chapters 4–6 (vision, code, cause) provide the content fathers must impart; Chapters 7–10 demonstrate how ceremonies seal that content.

**Key ideas:** Fatherhood requires intentional *definition, process and ceremony*; telling a boy to “be a man” without guidance is ineffective; rites of passage anchor masculine identity.

### Chapter 2 – **The Invisible Dad**

**Key question:** What damage does an emotionally or physically absent father inflict on a son’s development?

**Summary:** Lewis recounts his own upbringing with an alcoholic father who missed his wedding and rarely engaged with his sons【316853119993767†L618-L714】.  He describes the pain of growing up with a dad who was “invisible”: seldom playing, saying “I love you,” offering guidance or modeling spiritual leadership【316853119993767†L689-L705】.  The chapter explores how a father’s passivity and addiction cripple boys, leaving them to navigate adolescence alone and leading to bitterness, shame and confusion.  Lewis contends that providing materially is not enough; sons need emotional presence, affection and instruction to flourish.

**Examples:** *The author recounts Christmases ruined by his father’s drinking and the embarrassment of watching him stumble through the house*【316853119993767†L643-L657】.  He describes feeling forced to mediate parental conflicts and hiding car keys to prevent drunken accidents【316853119993767†L669-L679】.  A photo from his youth shows his father literally standing in the shadows, symbolizing his absence【316853119993767†L726-L730】.

**Related chapters:** Chapter 3 discusses how fatherlessness causes sons to drift; Chapter 12 (“The Decree”) stresses that only a father can bestow manhood.

**Key ideas:** Emotional absence wounds sons as deeply as physical absence; fathers must engage beyond provision; children internalize a father’s presence or neglect.

### Chapter 3 – **The Drift of Sons**

**Key question:** What happens to boys when fathers fail to guide them?

**Summary:** Drawing on sociological data and the story of serial killer Jeffrey Dahmer, Lewis explains that boys without engaged fathers “begin to sink into themselves” and drift into isolation and destructive behavior【316853119993767†L917-L930】.  He cites anthropologist Bronislaw Malinowski, who wrote that the father is indispensable for a child’s full sociological status【316853119993767†L919-L924】.  Fatherlessness, whether through divorce, workaholism or emotional detachment, leaves sons without moral anchors and contributes to social problems such as crime and violence.  The chapter emphasizes that fathers are the “glory” of their sons, a source of beauty and pride【316853119993767†L951-L965】, and that sons naturally crave their fathers’ admiration and guidance.

**Examples:** Lewis analyses Lionel Dahmer’s memoir *A Father’s Story*, noting that Jeffrey Dahmer’s descent into horror followed years of parental neglect and conflict【316853119993767†L905-L924】.  He also quotes Bo Jackson, who lamented that his father never watched him play professional sports【316853119993767†L970-L977】.

**Related chapters:** Chapter 2 provides a personal illustration of fatherly absence; Chapter 11 stresses the need for a community of men to support fathers and sons.

**Key ideas:** Fatherless sons drift towards isolation and moral confusion; a boy’s “glory” is his father; society suffers when fathers abdicate their roles.

### Chapter 4 – **A Vision for Manhood**

**Key question:** How can fathers recover a compelling vision of manhood for their sons?

**Summary:** Using the medieval knight William Marshall as a model, Lewis explores how chivalric culture trained boys to be men through mentorship, moral instruction and ceremony【316853119993767†L1190-L1204】. He notes that boys become men in the presence of a clear, culturally supported vision【316853119993767†L1232-L1237】.  Lewis proposes a definition of manhood based on the lives of Adam and Jesus: a real man **rejects passivity**, **accepts responsibility**, **leads courageously**, and **expects the greater reward—God’s reward**【316853119993767†L1616-L1623】.  Fathers must embrace this vision themselves and teach it to their sons at puberty【316853119993767†L1637-L1646】.

**Examples:** Lewis recounts the life of William Marshall, who was apprenticed to his cousin and taught the art of knighthood, courage, and loyalty【316853119993767†L1189-L1196】. **Related chapters:** Chapter 5 (code of conduct) spells out the moral boundaries; Chapter 6 introduces a transcendent cause that gives men purpose beyond themselves.

**Key ideas:** A clear vision for manhood is essential; boys need mentors and a cultural framework; manhood is defined by rejecting passivity, accepting responsibility, leading courageously and seeking God’s reward.

### Chapter 5 – **A Code of Conduct**

**Summary:** Lewis insists that a man without a moral compass is easily corrupted. **Examples:** The chapter opens with Lewis recounting his own shame when a teacher caught him cheating and told him, “You are what you are when no one is looking”【316853119993767†L1671-L1678】.  He also shares James P. Lenfestey’s “Catch of a Lifetime” story in which a father makes his son release a large bass because fishing season has not yet begun【316853119993767†L1684-L1700】.

**Related chapters:** Chapter 4 defines manhood principles; Chapter 7 demonstrates how ceremonies can reinforce moral lessons.

**Key ideas:** Moral boundaries must be taught early; integrity is doing right when no one is watching; fathers’ ethical instruction becomes a son’s internal compass.

**Examples:** The author quotes the Roman emperor Septimus, who lamented that life’s rewards were worth nothing【316853119993767†L2170-L2177】, and cites King Solomon’s reflections on the futility of wealth and pleasure【316853119993767†L2219-L2233】. **Related chapters:** Chapter 4 provides the vision; Chapter 5 supplies the code; Chapter 10 discusses ceremonies for commemorating a transcendent commitment.

**Key ideas:** Success without meaning breeds despair; boys need a cause that is heroic, timeless and divinely rooted; Christian faith offers the ultimate transcendent cause.

### Chapter 7 – **The Power of Ceremony**

### Chapter 8 – **Four Key Manhood Ceremonies**

Fathers should personalize ceremonies and allow God to use them in unexpected ways.

**Examples:** Lewis describes creating a family crest with two friends and using it during ceremonies to symbolise their values【316853119993767†L2869-L2885】.  He emphasizes that manhood ceremonies produce awe and unexpected depth in sons’ lives【316853119993767†L2851-L2866】.
