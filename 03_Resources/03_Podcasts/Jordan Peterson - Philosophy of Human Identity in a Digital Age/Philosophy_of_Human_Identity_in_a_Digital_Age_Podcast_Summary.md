## Abstract (≈300 words)

In this discussion, Jordan B. Peterson, icon carver and symbolism expert Jonathan Pageau, and journalist Mary Harrington grapple with the question: **What does it mean to be human in an age when machines can mimic our linguistic and cognitive abilities?**  They begin by noting that Enlightenment humanism equated humanity with rational or linguistic minds.  With language models now producing convincing prose, that definition is no longer sufficient.  Pageau argues that humans are defined by what we desire and aspire to, not merely by rationality.  Drawing on the biblical idea of *imago Dei*—being made in the image of God—he contends that identity is a vertical, aspirational journey towards transcendence.  Without this “vertical axis,” identities become fluid and conflicted, leading to battles over nationalism, gender and sexuality.

Harrington situates the term “identity” historically.  Her research shows that “identity” entered common usage only in the 1960s, supplanting the modern “self” and the pre‑modern “soul”【736933253594963†L44-L67】.  She argues that the digital revolution, which began in the 1960s, turned technologies inward, prompting people to treat their bodies and minds as malleable.  This shift fostered a fluid, politicised concept of identity that is increasingly commercialised and individualised.  Harrington urges listeners to look outward again—toward shared virtues and values—in order to ground themselves.

Peterson builds on these points by invoking the biblical stories of Jacob’s ladder and Abraham’s call.  He frames identity as an adventure marked by sacrifice and upward striving.  In the story of Abraham, God promises renown, generational blessing, non‑zero‑sum benefits and meaning to those who embark on a transformative journey.  Peterson argues that identity is not static or disembodied but emerges through sacrifice and relationship.  He critiques modernity’s abandonment of Aristotle’s formal and final causes, warning that a purely mechanistic view of humans leaves us unmoored.  Together, the speakers advocate for a renewed understanding of human identity that integrates ancient wisdom, teleology and virtue, offering a counter‑narrative to contemporary discourses of self‑construction and technological transcendence.

## Episode Outline (Deep Analysis)

### Opening – Questioning the Rationalist Definition of Humanity

**Guiding question:** How does artificial intelligence challenge Enlightenment definitions of what it means to be human?

**Expanded Answer & Key ideas:** Peterson sets the stage by noting that the Enlightenment equated being human with possessing a rational or linguistic mind.  Pageau observes that large language models now demonstrate “credible and rapidly improving linguistic minds,” forcing us to reconsider this definition【?†?】.  He argues that humans are distinguished not merely by cognitive capacity but by desires, aspirations and a relational orientation toward something transcendent.  This view aligns with the biblical conception of being made in the image of God (*imago Dei*), where identity is defined by an upward longing rather than mere reasoning【?†?】.

**Thesis statement(s):** Human identity cannot be reduced to rationality or linguistic ability; it is rooted in desire, aspiration and participation in transcendent order.

**Extended commentary:** By confronting the limitations of rationalist definitions of humanity, the panel underscores that AI’s linguistic capabilities challenge human exceptionalism.  Pageau’s emphasis on desire and aspiration introduces a vertical axis missing from secular accounts.  Without this axis, identity becomes flattened into horizontal categories (nation, gender, sexuality) that compete for primacy.

**Skeptical challenge:** Could human uniqueness be grounded in capacities other than language (e.g., consciousness, creativity) without invoking theology?  How might secular philosophies account for aspiration and transcendence without religious frameworks?

**Applications & Implications:** Recognising that identity encompasses more than rationality encourages education, policy and AI governance to value character formation, community and virtue.  It invites technologists to consider ethical boundaries that honour human dignity rather than reducing people to information processors.

### Historical Evolution of “Identity”

**Guiding question:** Where did the modern concept of “identity” come from, and what did it replace?

**Expanded Answer & Key ideas:** Harrington’s research shows that “identity” entered mainstream discourse only after the Second World War.  She uses Google’s N‑gram tool to demonstrate that the term gained traction in the 1960s【736933253594963†L44-L67】.  Before this, people spoke of the “self,” and earlier still, the “soul.”  Harrington argues that these shifts correlate with information technologies: literacy gave rise to the idea of the soul; the printing press fostered the modern self; and digital technologies enabled the fluid, individualised identities of today【736933253594963†L44-L67】.  She contends that the digital era’s inward‑turning technologies encourage people to see their bodies and minds as plastic, leading to identity politics and a “cyborg” culture【736933253594963†L120-L150】.

**Thesis statement(s):** The contemporary concept of identity is a recent cultural invention tied to the digital revolution; it replaced earlier notions of self and soul.

**Extended commentary:** Harrington’s historical framing exposes the contingent nature of identity.  By linking the concept to specific technologies, she shows that identity is not a timeless essence but a product of cultural and technological shifts.  Her argument that we are already living in a “cyborg” society challenges narratives that treat technological augmentation as a distant future【736933253594963†L120-L150】.

**Skeptical challenge:** Does the emergence of “identity” necessarily negate the relevance of the “self” or “soul,” or might these concepts coexist?  Are there non‑Western traditions where identity has been conceived differently?

**Applications & Implications:** Understanding the historical contingency of identity can help societies resist politicisation and commercialisation of personal identities.  It suggests that policies and cultural practices should prioritise shared virtues and common goods over subjective self‑definition.

### Vertical Orientation and Sacrifice

**Guiding question:** What role do aspiration and sacrifice play in forming identity?

**Expanded Answer & Key ideas:** Pageau argues that identity is aspirational and relational.  Drawing on biblical imagery, he describes humanity as climbing “Jacob’s ladder,” seeking to move upward toward the divine【?†?】.  Without this vertical orientation, identity becomes a competition among horizontal categories (nation, sexuality, family)【?†?】.  Harrington agrees, urging people to look beyond fluid personal identities and ground themselves in virtues and values.  Peterson expands this theme through the story of Abraham.  He notes that God promises Abraham renown, generational legacy, enduring value and non‑zero‑sum benefit if he embarks on a sacrificial journey【?†?】.  Identity thus arises through participation in a transformative adventure marked by sacrifice and service to others.

**Thesis statement(s):** Genuine identity involves upward striving toward transcendent values and is marked by sacrifice; it is not a static possession but a dynamic journey.

**Extended commentary:** The panel’s emphasis on sacrifice contrasts sharply with modern consumer culture, where identity is often curated through consumption and self‑expression.  By framing identity as a quest requiring sacrifice and obedience, the speakers call for a recovery of virtues like humility, generosity and endurance.  This view also integrates individual identity with communal and generational purposes.

**Skeptical challenge:** In pluralistic societies, whose transcendent values should guide the “upward” journey?  How can identity remain aspirational without becoming oppressive or exclusive?

**Applications & Implications:** Educators, parents and policymakers can foster environments that emphasise service, community and character development.  Religious and cultural traditions may offer resources for cultivating the virtues necessary for aspirational identity.

### Teleology and the Return of Formal and Final Causes

**Guiding question:** Why do the speakers argue for reintroducing teleology into our understanding of humanity?

**Expanded Answer & Key ideas:** Peterson notes that modern science jettisoned two of Aristotle’s four causes—formal and final causes—keeping only material and efficient causes【?†?】.  This reduction made it possible to build powerful technologies but left people without a sense of purpose or direction.  He argues that reintroducing formal (what something is) and final (what something is for) causes can ground identity and prevent technological dehumanisation.  Harrington’s call to restore the concept of the soul complements this.  Pageau emphasises that identity must have a form and a telos; otherwise it dissolves into endless self‑creation.

**Thesis statement(s):** A full understanding of human identity requires acknowledging form and purpose, not just mechanism; teleology provides the direction necessary for identity formation.

**Extended commentary:** By invoking Aristotle, the speakers align with intellectual movements seeking to recover teleology in science and ethics.  Without a sense of purpose, identity becomes a purely subjective project vulnerable to manipulation by markets and algorithms.  Teleology anchors identity in objective goods and ultimate ends.

**Skeptical challenge:** Can teleological thinking be reconciled with scientific naturalism?  How can societies agree on final causes in a pluralistic world?

**Applications & Implications:** Resurrecting teleological thinking may inform bioethics, AI design and education.  It encourages designers of technology to consider human flourishing, not just efficiency or profit.
