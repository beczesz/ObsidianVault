## Host Notes & Interview Strategy


Medical interviews require Levi to embody the intelligent patient—not expert, not naive, but genuinely curious and appropriately skeptical. The interview strategy has several layers:

**Asking Practical Questions:** Levi's first job is to ask the questions listeners actually have. Not theoretical questions, but practical ones. "What should someone actually do if they face this situation?" "What's the most common mistake you see patients making?" "How do you advise someone who doesn't trust the conventional approach?" These questions serve the audience. They're not showing off knowledge. They're answering real needs.

**Following Up on Jargon:** Medical communication often uses terminology that physicians understand but patients don't. Levi's job is to notice jargon and ask for translation. "Can you explain what prophylaxis means in simpler terms?" "When you say the protocol is X, what does that mean for someone who actually has to live with it?" This is not dumb question—it's intelligent clarification that makes expert knowledge accessible.

**Grounding Abstract Information:** Medical information often stays abstract without stories. "The risk is 5%" doesn't land the same way as "Let me tell you about a patient who faced exactly this decision. Here's what happened." Levi's strategy is to ask for stories that ground abstract information. Stories create empathy. Statistics create understanding. Both are needed.

**Maintaining Independent Editorial Voice:** Respect for expertise doesn't mean deferring to it completely. Levi can ask hard questions. "Is there research supporting this?" "What would you say to someone who disagrees?" "What are the limitations of what we know?" These questions aren't adversarial. They're honest. They serve both the guest (giving opportunity to address concerns) and the audience (modeling how to think critically about medical information).

**Building Trust:** The deepest work in medical interviews is trust-building. This happens when: (1) The doctor acknowledges uncertainty—"I don't know" becomes okay; (2) The doctor prioritizes patient understanding—willingness to repeat or rephrase; (3) The doctor shares why they believe what they believe—moving beyond authority to reasoning; (4) The doctor acknowledges when they've changed their mind—showing intellectual growth, not defending old positions.

**Medical Strategy:** The interview goal is not to make listeners experts. It's to make them informed participants in their own healthcare. Listeners should leave thinking: "I understand the basic facts. I know what questions to ask my own doctor. I trust this person because they're honest about uncertainty and focused on my understanding, not their authority."
