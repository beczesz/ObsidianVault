Why One DevOps Engineer Is Never Enough

The infrastructure demands of modern cloud environments have outgrown the single-engineer model. Here’s what most companies only discover after something breaks. 

Picture this: your first DevOps hire has been with you for eight months. In that time, they’ve migrated your infrastructure to AWS, set up your CI/CD pipeline, containerised your application in Kubernetes, and built a monitoring stack you’re finally proud of. Things are running smoothly. Then they go on holiday. 
On day three, a deployment fails. The pipeline throws an error no one else has seen before. Your backend is down. You’re staring at a Terraform state file and a Slack thread growing by the minute, and the one person who knows what any of it means is offline, sitting on a beach somewhere, completely unreachable. 

This is not a hypothetical. We’ve seen it happen to companies of all sizes, across all industries. And the problem is rarely the engineer. The problem is structural: modern infrastructure is too broad, too complex, and too demanding for any single person to cover sustainably, regardless of their skill level. 

 

The Myth of the Full-Stack DevOps Engineer 

If you’ve recently posted a DevOps job description, or read one, you’ll recognise the pattern immediately. The typical listing asks for expertise in AWS and GCP, proficiency with Kubernetes and Terraform, hands-on experience with CI/CD pipelines (Jenkins, GitHub Actions, CircleCI, take your pick), solid knowledge of observability tooling like Prometheus, Grafana, and Datadog, an understanding of network security and zero-trust architecture, a grip on compliance frameworks such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001, and ideally some background in cost optimisation and FinOps. 

But let’s be honest! That’s not a job description. That’s a wishlist for six different engineers. 

The reality is that even the most experienced DevOps professionals genuinely specialise in two or three of those domains. A senior infrastructure engineer who is exceptional at AWS networking and Terraform may not have deep Kubernetes internals experience. Someone who lives and breathes CI/CD and developer tooling may not be the right person to own your security posture or lead a compliance audit. 

This is not a weakness in your hiring. It’s the nature of the field. Cloud infrastructure has fragmented into many specialties over the past decade, and the breadth of what “DevOps” encompasses has grown faster than any individual career path can keep pace with. 

 

This isn’t just anecdotal frustration. Multiple industry hiring reports show sustained demand outpacing supply in DevOps and cloud infrastructure roles, with a significant percentage of positions remaining open for extended periods. Broader IT workforce data consistently indicates that most employers struggle to find candidates with the required cloud, automation, and security depth. In other words, the “unicorn” full-stack DevOps engineer isn’t rare because companies are picky. They’re rare because the market simply doesn’t produce enough professionals who can cover every domain at expert level. 

 

Consider the surface area a properly functioning DevOps function needs to cover: 

  • Infrastructure provisioning and cloud architecture (AWS, GCP, Azure) 
  • Container orchestration and Kubernetes cluster management 
  • CI/CD pipeline design, maintenance, and developer experience 
  • Observability: logging, metrics, alerting, and on-call response 
  • Security hardening, IAM policies, and vulnerability management 
  • Compliance, audit readiness, and documentation 
  • Cloud cost management and resource optimisation 
  • Disaster recovery planning and runbook maintenance 
  • Strategic architecture oversight and capacity planning 

A single hire will inevitably cover some of this well and leave gaps elsewhere. Those gaps don’t feel dangerous at first. They become dangerous later, at the worst possible moment. 

 

The Risks You Don’t See Until It’s Too Late 

The single-engineer model creates four categories of risk that most teams only fully understand in hindsight. 

Knowledge silos and tribal memory 

When one person builds the infrastructure, one person understands it. The architecture decisions that seemed obvious at the time rarely get documented. The workaround that was applied eighteen months ago when a deployment broke isn’t in any runbook. The reason a particular security group rule exists the way it does lives entirely in someone’s memory. 

This is what the industry calls the “bus factor” or “lottery factor”: how many people would need to be hit by a bus (or, more cheerfully, win the lottery and quit) for your infrastructure to become unmanageable? In the single-engineer model, the answer is one. That’s an extremely fragile position for any company that depends on its systems being available. 

Burnout and turnover 

DevOps engineers shoulder a disproportionate operational burden in most organisations. They’re the ones on call when alerts fire at 3AM. They’re the ones interrupted mid-sprint to investigate a production anomaly. They absorb the developer requests, the security queries, the finance team’s cost questions, and the CEO’s sudden interest in uptime after reading a newsletter. 

 

Recent engineering surveys show that burnout remains one of the most common challenges in infrastructure and operations roles, with a majority of practitioners reporting sustained stress from on-call demands and operational pressure. DevOps functions sit at the intersection of development speed and production stability, two forces that often conflict. When a single individual carries that tension alone, week after week, the risk is not just fatigue. It’s debilitation. 

 

Industry data shows the average DevOps engineer stays in a role for about 1-2 years, with only a third remaining 5-7 years, thus reinforcing how high turnover compounds knowledge risk. 

Technology roles see high turnover and skill pressure, often linked to burnout and opportunity seeking. Recruiting, onboarding, and rebuilding institutional knowledge takes months and costs significantly more than most companies budget for. And when the engineer leaves, those knowledge silos we described above leave together with them. 

Coverage gaps: nights, weekends, and annual leave 

Infrastructure doesn’t respect office hours. A sustainable on-call rotation requires at minimum three to four engineers to avoid any individual being on-call more than one week in four. With a single DevOps engineer, your on-call coverage is effectively one person, all the time, indefinitely. 

That’s not a sustainable arrangement, and most experienced engineers know it. Many will accept it for a period, but it becomes a significant factor in their decision to look elsewhere. 

Career stagnation and professional isolation 

Engineers grow by working alongside other engineers. A solo DevOps hire has no one to review their architecture decisions, challenge their approach, or introduce them to patterns and tooling they haven’t encountered before. Over time, this leads to technical plateauing and a gradual disengagement from the role. It’s one of the quieter factors behind that 18-to-24-month tenure figure. 

 

What a Resilient DevOps Function Actually Looks Like 

If the single-engineer model is structurally insufficient, what does “enough” actually look like? In our experience, a properly functioning DevOps capability requires four things running simultaneously. 

First, a primary infrastructure engineer with deep familiarity with your specific environment. Second, a secondary engineer with overlapping knowledge. Not a full duplicate, but enough cross-training to act confidently under pressure. Third, an on-call rotation of at minimum two to three people, so that no single person is carrying that load every week. Fourth, architectural oversight: someone who is thinking six months ahead about scalability, cost, security posture, and compliance, rather than just responding to the present. 

For most growing companies, the honest answer is that maintaining all four of those things with in-house hires requires a minimum of three DevOps engineers. That’s a significant commitment in salary, recruitment overhead, and management capacity, particularly for companies that are still scaling their product. 

 

This aligns with findings from long-running DevOps performance research programs, which consistently show that high-performing technology organisations rely on shared practices, documentation, and team-level resilience rather than individual heroics. Infrastructure reliability is strongly correlated with collaboration, clear ownership boundaries, and knowledge distribution, not with having a single highly capable engineer operating in isolation. Performance at scale is a team property. 

 

This is why the managed service model exists and why it makes practical sense for companies at a certain stage of growth. 

 

The Practical Alternatives Worth Considering 

There isn’t a universal answer. The right DevOps staffing strategy depends on your size, maturity, regulatory pressure, and growth trajectory. But in practice, most companies land in one of three models. 

Option A: Build an in-house team 

To remove single DevOps engineer risk properly, you need depth. In practical terms, that means at least three DevOps engineers to ensure knowledge redundancy, sustainable on-call rotation, and architectural oversight. 

At current Western European market rates, that represents a minimum commitment of €200,000–€300,000 per year in salaries alone. And that is before recruitment fees, onboarding time, benefits, tooling, and management overhead. 

This model makes sense at scale. Companies with 200+ engineers, strict compliance requirements, or highly complex infrastructure often need this level of internal ownership. But below that scale, the fixed cost can be disproportionate to actual infrastructure demand.  

Option B: Managed DevOps service  

A managed DevOps partner provides access to a team rather than an individual: shared knowledge, documented processes, cross-coverage, and built-in redundancy. Typical costs range between €2,000 to €6,000 per month depending on scope and coverage. That is significantly less than building equivalent capability in-house.  

For startups (5-50 employees), this is almost always the most rational solution. Infrastructure needs are real, but not yet large enough to justify three full-time hires. Managed services provide senior-level expertise, on-call coverage, and architectural oversight from day one. For many mid-sized companies (50-500 employees), this model continues to make financial and operational sense, particularly if infrastructure complexity is growing faster than the internal team. The key requirement: transparency. You should always retain visibility and control over your own systems.     

Option C: Hybrid model 

The hybrid model keeps your internal DevOps engineer focused on day-to-day enablement, developer support, and company-specific context, while a managed service partner provides architectural guidance, specialty expertise, and shared on-call coverage. 

For mid-sized companies, this is often the most balanced structure. It reduces the infrastructure bus factor without requiring an immediate three-hire commitment. 

For large enterprises (500+ employees), hybrid becomes complementary rather than substitutive, external partners strengthen internal teams, fill skill gaps, and provide surge capacity during transformation projects. 

 

The Strategic Principle: Plan Before the Incident  

The common mistake is waiting for a resignation, an outage, or a compliance deadline before addressing DevOps team size and redundancy. Resilient infrastructure isn’t built reactively. It’s designed intentionally, with overlap, documentation, and coverage in place before they’re urgently needed. The single-engineer model can work temporarily. However, it is rarely a sustainable long-term strategy. 

 

The Takeaway 

Hiring your first DevOps engineer is a genuinely positive step. It means your infrastructure is being taken seriously, and in the early stages it will likely feel like enough. 

But it’s a starting point, not a strategy. A strategy accounts for what happens when that person is unavailable, when they leave, when your compliance requirements change, when your architecture needs to evolve. A strategy includes redundancy, knowledge distribution, and sustainable coverage. 

We’ve worked with companies who reached out after an outage, after a resignation, after realising their infrastructure had become a single point of failure. We’d much rather step-in and help companies build resilience before any of those things happen.
